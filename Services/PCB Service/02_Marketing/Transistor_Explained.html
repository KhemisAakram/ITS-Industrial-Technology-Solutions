What is a Transistor?

1What is a Transistor?

A transistor is a tiny electronic component that acts like a switch or an amplifier. It's the fundamental building block of all modern electronics — computers, phones, radios, industrial controllers, everything.

Think of it as a tap: a small turn of the handle (base current) controls a huge flow of water (collector-emitter current).

Invented in 1947 at Bell Labs, the transistor replaced bulky vacuum tubes and made electronics small, efficient, and reliable. A modern CPU contains billions of transistors on a chip smaller than your thumbnail.

2How Does It Work?

A transistor has 3 terminals: Base (B), Collector (C), and Emitter (E).

In an NPN transistor (most common):

  • A small current flowing from Base → Emitter turns the transistor ON
  • This allows a much larger current to flow from Collector → Emitter
  • No base current = no collector current = transistor is OFF
  • A small input controls a big output — that's amplification!

3Transistor as a Switch (Most Common Use)

This is how transistors are used in industrial electronics — to turn things ON and OFF based on a control signal.

4How a Transistor Thinks

🔖

Base (B) — The Gatekeeper

A tiny signal here decides if the big current flows or not. No signal = OFF.

⚡

Collector (C) — The Power In

This is where the main current enters. Think of it as the water pipe inlet.

🚀

Emitter (E) — The Power Out

The current exits here. When Base is ON, Collector→Emitter flows freely.

📈

Gain (hFE) — Amplification Factor

A typical transistor has gain of 100-800. 1mA at Base can control 100mA at Collector!

5Real-World PCB Examples

Here's where you'll find transistors in industrial boards:

  • Power supply boards — Switching transistors in SMPS circuits (MOSFETs)
  • Relay drivers — A transistor amplifies a PLC signal to energize a relay coil
  • Sensor inputs — Transistor buffers protect the controller from voltage spikes
  • Motor speed control — Power transistors (IGBTs) in VFD drives
  • Signal amplifiers — Amplify weak thermocouple or RTD signals
When repairing PCBs, the most common transistor faults are: shorted C-E (stuck ON), open B-E junction (stuck OFF), or leaky C-E (partial ON). All easily checked with a multimeter.
